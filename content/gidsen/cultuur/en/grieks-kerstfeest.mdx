\*Christmas on Crete differs in many ways from how the holiday is experienced in Belgium and the 
Netherlands. While it isn't a lavish or commercial spectacle, Christmas does play a visible and 
meaningful role in daily life. The Christmas period here begins noticeably early and unfolds at a 
calmer pace, with a strong focus on faith, family and tradition.\*

\*Anyone visiting Crete in winter, or staying here for longer, quickly notices that Christmas isn't 
an isolated moment, but part of a broader winter period in which the island takes on a different 
rhythm.\*

## When Is Christmas Celebrated in Greece?

Contrary to what's often assumed, Greeks don't celebrate Christmas on January 7th, but simply on 
December 25th. For Christmas, the Greek Orthodox Church follows a calendar that aligns with the 
Gregorian calendar.

Preparations already begin on November 15th, with a forty-day fasting period. Traditionally, meat, 
dairy and eggs were avoided during this time. Today, not everyone still follows this fast strictly, 
but the idea of moderation and preparation remains present, especially among older generations and 
in villages.

The most important day is Christmas Day itself. Christmas Eve is usually quiet; the focus is on 
attending church and the family meal on December 25th.

## Christmas Decorations and the Streetscape

In Greece, the Christmas period noticeably starts earlier than in Belgium and the Netherlands. 
Because there's no Sinterklaas tradition, Christmas is the first major winter celebration. As early 
as the beginning of November, Christmas lights, decorated squares and sometimes even Christmas 
trees start appearing in towns and villages.

In places like [Heraklion](/gidsen/heraklion/bezoek-heraklion), 
[Rethymno](/gidsen/rethymnon/bezoek-rethymno) and [Chania](/gidsen/chania/musea-van-chania), you'll 
see lit-up streets, a central Christmas tree on the main square, and shops adjusting their window 
displays. The decorations tend to stay understated, but are clearly present. In smaller villages 
too, lights start appearing from November around cafés, churches and village squares.

That early start means the Christmas period grows gradually, without the rushed feeling you might 
experience in some other countries.

## The Religious Meaning of Christmas on Crete

Within the Orthodox tradition, Christmas is first and foremost about the birth of Christ. The 
emphasis is on spiritual meaning, not on decoration or gifts. Church attendance therefore plays a 
central role, especially on the morning of December 25th.

In villages, the church also serves as a meeting place. Families come together, wish each other a 
good holiday, and then head home for the meal. Compared to Easter, Greece's most important 
religious holiday, Christmas is celebrated more modestly, but it remains an essential moment in the 
church calendar.

## The Opening of the Christmas Season in Athens

Every year, the Christmas season in Greece is festively opened at Syntagma Square in Athens, in 
front of the parliament building. The lighting of the Christmas illuminations and the large 
Christmas tree marks a symbolic starting point for the winter festivities and receives a lot of 
media attention.

A large, richly decorated Christmas tree stands on the square, surrounded by light installations 
and music. For many Greeks, this moment marks the beginning of the Christmas season, even though 
the first lights often appear in shops and streets earlier. The atmosphere is distinctly festive 
and draws both locals and visitors.

## Agios Vasilis and Gift-Giving

On Crete, gifts are usually not given at Christmas, but on New Year's Day. That tradition is tied 
to Agios Vasilis (Saint Basil), a historical 4th-century bishop known for his generosity and care 
for the poor.

Agios Vasilis is not a commercial figure like Santa Claus in the West, but a saint with moral and 
religious significance. For children, New Year's Day is therefore often more important than 
Christmas itself.

## The Illuminated Christmas Boat (το καραβάκι)

Alongside the Christmas tree, Greece has an older Christmas tradition: the illuminated Christmas 
boat, the karaváki. Instead of a tree, a small wooden boat was traditionally decorated with lights 
and ornaments, especially in coastal areas and on islands.

This tradition has its roots in Greece's maritime character. The boat symbolizes a safe return, 
hope and a connection to the sea. In families with seafarers, the karaváki was seen as a wish for 
protection and prosperity during long voyages. The light stood as a symbol of guidance and safety.

Today, you mainly see the Christmas boat in public decorations: on squares, in harbors, at town 
halls, or as a light installation along the coast. Sometimes it's an actual boat covered in lights, 
sometimes a stylized silhouette made of light. In cities like Athens, the karaváki regularly appears 
alongside — or even instead of — a classic Christmas tree.

On Crete, you'll mostly see this tradition in coastal towns and harbors, where illuminated boats or 
ship-shaped light motifs are part of the Christmas decorations. The custom is less dominant than it 
used to be, but remains a recognizable and typically Greek element of the Christmas period.

## Christmas Greetings in Greek

During the Christmas and New Year period, you'll hear several simple greetings in Greece, often 
used interchangeably. They may sound similar, but each carries its own nuance.

- Καλά Χριστούγεννα (Kalá Christoúgenna) – Literally "Good Christmas." This greeting is used 
specifically around Christmas Day itself, on both December 24th and 25th.
- Χρόνια Πολλά (Chrónia Pollá) – Means "many years [to come]." It's a broad greeting used for 
various holidays, including Christmas, name days and New Year's. It's probably the most commonly 
heard greeting during this period.
- Καλές γιορτές (Kalés yortés) – Literally "happy holidays." This greeting is mainly used 
throughout the whole period from early December to after New Year's, when referring not to one 
specific holiday but to the festive season in general.

In practice, you'll hear these greetings everywhere: in shops, cafés, with neighbors and during 
family visits. One short greeting is enough; elaborate phrasing isn't customary.

## Typical Christmas Dishes on Crete

After the fasting period, Christmas also marks a culinary turning point. There's no fixed national 
Christmas menu, but on Crete certain traditional dishes do reappear around Christmas.

Meat once again plays an important role. Common dishes include:

- Χοιρινό στον φούρνο (choirinó ston foúrno) – oven-roasted pork with potatoes, lemon and oregano
- Αρνί ή κατσίκι (arní or katsíki) – lamb or goat, slow-cooked
- Κοκκινιστό (kokkinistó) – stewed meat in tomato sauce, sometimes with cinnamon or cloves

On Crete, regional dishes also appear on the table, such as:

- Αντικριστό (antikristo) – slow-grilled lamb, especially in mountain areas
- Χοχλιοί (chochlíoi) – snails, often prepared with rosemary or in tomato sauce

The meal is rounded out with local cheeses like graviera and myzithra, bread, olives and seasonal 
vegetables.

### Christopsomo – The Christmas Bread

An important symbolic element is Christopsomo (Χριστόψωμο), the traditional Christmas bread. The 
bread is decorated with a cross or dough motifs referring to the family or the family's trade. On 
Christmas Day, it's cut and shared, usually by the oldest family member.

### Vasilopita: The Bread with the Lucky Coin

Around New Year's, [Vasilopita (Βασιλόπιτα)](/recepten/vasilopita) is traditionally cut in Greece 
and on Crete — a festive bread or cake with a coin (flouri) hidden inside. The custom is tied to 
Agios Vasilis, whose name day is celebrated on January 1st.

When the Vasilopita is cut, each family member is given a piece. Whoever finds the coin in their 
slice is said to have luck and prosperity in the new year. A slice is often also cut for the house 
itself, for Christ, or for someone who's absent.

The Vasilopita is usually cut at midnight or on New Year's Day, often after a short blessing. In 
families, cafés, associations and even workplaces, this ritual is still part of starting the new 
year. The shape and flavor vary by region: sometimes it's a bread, sometimes more of a cake, often 
flavored with orange or mastiha.

Although this custom is often mentioned alongside Christmas, strictly speaking it's part of the New 
Year's traditions, not Christmas Day itself.

### Sweets of the Christmas Season

From early December, the offerings in bakeries visibly change. Two sweets are inseparably linked to 
Christmas:

- Μελομακάρονα ([melomakarona](/recepten/melomakarona)) – honey cookies with walnuts and orange
- Κουραμπιέδες (kourabiedes) – butter cookies with almonds and powdered sugar

On Crete, you'll also find local sweets, such as:

- Ξεροτήγανα (xerotígana) – fried dough with honey and nuts
- Λυχναράκια (lychnarákia) – pastries filled with sweet myzithra

These treats are served with coffee and during family visits, often throughout the entire Christmas 
period.

## Christmas Markets and Events

Crete doesn't have extensive Christmas markets like those in Northern Europe. There are, however, 
local initiatives:

- In [Heraklion](/gidsen/heraklion/bezoek-heraklion), a temporary Christmas village is often set up 
on or around Liberty Square
- [Chania](/gidsen/chania/musea-van-chania) and [Rethymno](/gidsen/rethymnon/bezoek-rethymno) 
usually have limited stalls and lighting

These events are mainly aimed at local families and children and remain small-scale.

## In Closing

Christmas on Crete is visibly present, but remains simple in substance. The decorations start 
early, the traditions run deep, and the emphasis is on faith and togetherness. For anyone wanting 
to get to know Crete outside of high season, the Christmas period is a quiet and meaningful time.
