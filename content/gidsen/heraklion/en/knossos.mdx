\*On a gently rolling hill (Kephala), just south of modern-day Heraklion, lie the remains of a 
city that was once the beating heart of an extraordinary civilization. Knossos is not just a 
ruin: it's a window into the Minoan world — the early culture that produced Europe's first 
urban complexes.\*

## Minos Kalokairinos: The Local Discoverer

Long before archaeologists from abroad arrived, residents of Crete already knew the hill as a 
place full of old stones and pottery shards. In 1878, the Cretan merchant and antiquarian Minos 
Kalokairinos decided to follow up on those stories. He carried out small excavations and 
discovered rows of large pithoi (storage jars) and parts of underground rooms — signs that 
something significant must be hidden there. His finds created the impression that he had 
touched the center of a palace-like structure.

But at that time, Crete was still under Ottoman rule, and he could not legally dig further. 
The discoveries were shelved, and the hill remained largely untouched — until the next phase 
of scientific exploration began.

## Arthur Evans: Vision, Ambition and Controversy

Around the turn of the century, the British archaeologist Arthur Evans traveled to Crete, 
intrigued by inscriptions and stories about ancient seal stones. He recognized the potential 
significance of the hill near [Heraklion](/en/gidsen/heraklion/bezoek-heraklion), bought the land 
from Kalokairinos in 1900, and began systematic excavations. Within a short time, his team 
uncovered foundations, walls, courtyards and wall paintings. Evans introduced the name 
"Palace of Minos" and named the culture "Minoan" — a piece of imagination that strongly 
influenced both the public and scholars.

But Evans's reconstructions — he added to walls, used concrete, and rebuilt sections based on 
his own interpretation — have since been criticized as overly speculative. Today, researchers 
use digital techniques and critical study to try to distinguish original structures from later 
reconstructions.

## The Palace Complex of Knossos: Structure and Function

What you see at Knossos today isn't just a royal palace, but an extensive palace complex. It 
combined residential, administrative, religious and economic functions. There are courtyards, 
workshops, storage rooms, light and air shafts, colonnades, and corridors that at first glance 
seem maze-like.

Evans and later restorers managed to restore the central courtyard, the staircases, the throne 
room and parts of the frescoes. But because of their restorations, it's sometimes hard to tell 
what's authentic and what's interpretation — something modern archaeologists and digital models 
are currently working hard to untangle.

## The Minoan Culture

During its peak period (roughly 2000 to 1450 BC), Knossos was an administrative and cultural 
center. The Minoans controlled maritime routes across the Aegean and Mediterranean seas, and 
their art, architecture and symbolism influenced entire regions.

The mythology reflects this fascination with ritual and symbolism: King Minos, the bull, the 
labyrinth and the Minotaur are among the most famous stories that Knossos projected into the 
Greek imagination.

## The Fall of the Minoan Civilization

A frequently cited theory about the fall of the Minoan civilization involves the devastating 
eruption of the Santorini (Thera) volcano. That eruption is estimated to have occurred around 
1600–1500 BC. The explosion is thought to have caused a massive tsunami that battered Crete's 
north coast, destroying harbors and economic infrastructure.

Although the exact timing and impact are debated, it's clear that this disaster weakened Minoan 
society. Trade and harbors may never have fully recovered.

In the aftermath, the Mycenaean Greeks from the mainland were able to move in on the weakened 
Minoans. They used the network of Minoan centers, took over parts of the administration, and 
introduced their own pottery style and script (Linear B). Knossos remained in use, albeit as a 
Mycenaean center, and thus became a bridge between the Minoan and the classical Greek world.

Archaeologically, traces of burning, rebuilding and transitional phenomena can be seen across 
various palace complexes, reinforcing the idea of a gradual takeover.

## Other Palace Complexes on Crete

**Phaistos** — located in the [Messara Plain](/en/gidsen/heraklion/de-messaravlakte), famous for the 
Phaistos Disc and as a cultural center that may have been connected to Knossos.

**Malia** — on the north coast, the third-largest palace complex; excavations there also 
revealed evidence of tsunami layers following the Thera eruption.

**Zakros (Kato Zakros)** — on the east coast, with numerous workshops and a location close to 
the sea, making it interesting for trade research. It lies right near the exit of the 
[Zakros Gorge](/en/gidsen/natuur-en-wandelen/de-mooiste-kloven).

**Zominthos and Kydonia** — less frequently visited by tourists, but important in the 2025 
UNESCO recognition.

**Galatas** — a less-developed complex near Arkalochori / Heraklion, not yet open to visitors, 
but identified as a palace center from the Neopalatial period.
