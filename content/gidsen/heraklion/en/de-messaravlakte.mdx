One of our regular routes is from [Heraklion](/gidsen/heraklion/bezoek-heraklion) to the Messara

## The Messara Plain

The Messara Plain is the largest and most fertile plain on Crete. It lies in southern Crete, 
between the mountain ranges of Psiloritis and Asterousia. It's a quiet area with 
[olive groves](/gidsen/eten-en-drinken/bezoek-olijfboeren), small villages and lots of 
agriculture. Anyone driving through here sees the real, rural Crete: simplicity, tradition and 
hospitality.

## First Stop: Gortyna

The first stop is Gortyna, an archaeological site nestled among olive groves in the heart of the 
Messara. In antiquity, this was the second most important city on Crete after 
[Knossos](/gidsen/heraklion/knossos), and in Roman times it even became the capital of the 
province of Creta et Cyrenaica.

What you visit today is actually just a small part of the original city. The tourist section lies 
along the main road, where you can see the Gortyn Law Code — a wall bearing one of the oldest 
surviving Greek legal texts — along with the remains of a theater, baths and an early Christian 
basilica.

But those who look a bit further quickly notice that Gortyna is far larger than the fenced-off 
site. Cross the road and walk among the 
[olive trees](/gidsen/eten-en-drinken/bezoek-olijfboeren): columns, stones and fragments lie 
scattered everywhere among the greenery. Further east, in the middle of the groves, large Roman 
buildings still stand where archaeologists continue to work today. And for those with a drone: 
from the air, you can really see just how vast the ancient city once was.

## Second Stop: Moires

Moires is the largest town in the region. There isn't much to see on its own, unless there's a 
market (usually on Saturdays). Then it's a lively bustle: fruit, vegetables, herbs, clothing and 
plenty of chatter. On other days, you can simply stop for a coffee or a snack at a café terrace.

## Third Stop: Agia Marina Donkey Rescue

Just outside Moires lies the Agia Marina Donkey Rescue Centre, a place I'd recommend to everyone. 
It was founded in 2004 by Barbara and Fanis, a German-Greek couple with a big heart. They take in 
donkeys that have become old, sick, or simply "no longer needed." And believe me, there are far 
more of those than you'd think.

On Crete — as in many Southern European countries — donkeys are still often used as working 
animals. They carry heavy loads, walk in the heat, and once they can no longer keep up, they're 
often simply left to fend for themselves. Sometimes literally: tied to a tree, without food or 
water. That's the harsh side of an island where animals are mostly seen in functional terms.

Here at Agia Marina, they get a second life. You'll see them standing in the shade, with gentle 
eyes, calmly munching on hay. The atmosphere is simple and sincere. No frills, no tourist show — 
just people doing what's right. Dogs, cats, ducks and goats now live here too, all having found a 
new home.

Everything runs on donations, so every euro helps. You can make a donation through their website. 
If you're planning a visit, it's best to check their Facebook page first — it's sometimes closed 
due to extreme heat or flooding.

## Fourth Stop: Mystical View Kommos

The next stop is Mystical View, a taverna with a beautiful view over Kommos Beach. It's the ideal 
spot for a lunch break: great food, a relaxed atmosphere and a phenomenal sea view. If you have 
time, you can visit Kommos Beach afterward — expansive, quiet and usually less crowded than 
Matala.

## Fifth Stop: Listaros and Botano

The small village of Listaros sits a bit higher up, with a view over the plain. It's best known 
for Botano, a charming herb shop run by a local family. Inside, hundreds of glass jars hold tea, 
herbs, dried fruit, honey, oil and natural products. Even if you don't buy anything, it's fun just 
to look around.

The village also has a few taverns where you can grab a drink before continuing on.

## Sixth Stop: Matala

The day ends in Matala, one of the best-known spots in South Crete. Carved into the soft chalk 
cliffs around the bay are caves that have existed since the Neolithic period. During the Minoan 
and Roman eras, they served as tombs, and in the 1960s, hippies moved in to live there. Among them 
were artists like Joni Mitchell, Leonard Cohen, and likely Bob Dylan too. Mitchell wrote the song 
Carey here.

Today, Matala is a cheerful but busy village full of shops, music and tourists. The beach is 
beautiful, but very crowded in summer.

I have to be honest: I have a love-hate relationship with Matala. Every time, I think, "Let's go 
have a look." And every time, I turn the car around when I see the overflowing parking lot. It's 
busy. Always. Narrow streets full of people, shops, music from every corner — it's lively, but 
overwhelming.

Those interested in history can visit the caves, or drive a bit further to the Roman workshops 
just outside the village. There you can see basins carved into the rock where garum (fermented 
fish sauce) and salted fish were once made — along with salt extracted from seawater. Locally, 
they're known as "Αρχαία Εργαστήρια Ματάλων" — the Ancient Workshops of Matala.

Matala is a bit of everything at once: history, nostalgia, mass tourism and charm. Whether you 
love it depends on your mood that day — but it remains a place you should see at least once.
