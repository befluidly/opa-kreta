## Spinalonga on Crete: From Venetian Fortress to Leper Colony

\*Spinalonga is a small island in the bay of Mirabello, off the northeast coast of Crete, directly 
across from the village of Plaka. Despite the short distance to the mainland, the island long 
played the role of a closed-off world. Today Spinalonga is an important historical site, best 
known as a former leper colony, but its history goes back further and consists of several 
layers.\*

\*A visit to Spinalonga benefits from some context. Without that background, the island can 
quickly feel like just a collection of walls and ruins; with knowledge of its past, it becomes a 
place that speaks of fear, policy and human resilience.\*

### The End of a Defensive Stronghold

Spinalonga's first major construction phase dates from the Venetian period, at the end of the 
16th century. The Venetians fortified the island around 1579–1586 to protect the bay of Elounda 
from raids. The massive walls and bastions still standing today are the most visible result of 
that effort.

After the Ottoman conquest of Crete, Spinalonga remained inhabited and strategically relevant for 
some time. It wasn't until the end of the 19th century that the island lost its military 
significance. That physical isolation and existing infrastructure later made it suitable for a 
completely different purpose.

### Spinalonga as a Leper Colony (1904–1957)

In 1904, the Cretan state decided to isolate people with leprosy on Spinalonga. The disease was 
still poorly understood at the time and provoked great fear. Isolation was seen as protecting 
society, not as caring for the patient.

Over the 53 years the colony existed, an estimated 1,000 to 1,200 people lived on the island. The 
number of residents at any one time was usually lower, peaking around 300 patients. They came 
mainly from Crete, but also from other parts of Greece.

For many, arriving on Spinalonga meant a permanent break from their previous life. Contact with 
family was often limited or cut off entirely, possessions were left behind, and patients' social 
status all but disappeared.

The colony closed in 1957, after leprosy became treatable with antibiotics and isolation was no 
longer considered necessary.

### Life Within the Walls

Although Spinalonga began as a place of banishment, an organized community developed there over 
the years. Residents set up homes, opened small shops, and organized their daily lives as best 
they could within the imposed restrictions.

An important figure in this process was Epaminondas Remoundakis, a patient who campaigned for 
better living conditions and greater self-organization. Thanks to such initiatives, more 
facilities became available and life on the island grew more humane, though it remained hard and 
uncertain.

Spinalonga was not a passive waiting room for death. There were marriages, conflicts, friendships 
and routines. That's exactly what makes the island historically significant: it shows how people, 
even under forced isolation, try to maintain structure and dignity.

### What You Can See on Spinalonga Today

During a visit, you walk past the remains of houses and public buildings, connected by a central 
street. The church holds a recognizable place; religion played an important role in community 
life.

The Venetian walls offer views over the bay of Mirabello, while modest cemeteries lie at the edges 
of the island. These areas serve as a reminder that Spinalonga isn't just an archaeological site, 
but also a place where people lived and died.

### Spinalonga in a Broader Context

Spinalonga's story doesn't stand alone. Around the world, leprosy patients were isolated, often on 
islands. A well-known example is Molokai in Hawaii, where the Belgian priest Father Damien worked.

Father Damien, born Jozef De Veuster in 1840 in Tremelo, chose in 1873 to voluntarily go and live 
in the leper colony of Kalaupapa. He cared for the sick, built infrastructure, and deliberately 
lived among the patients. Eventually he contracted leprosy himself and died in 1889.

Where Spinalonga stands as a symbol of forced isolation, Damien is often seen as an example of 
voluntary solidarity. Yet both stories reveal the same core truth: leprosy was not just a medical 
condition, but above all a social condemnation.

For Belgian visitors, this story carries extra meaning. In 1936, Father Damien's body was 
transported back to Belgium aboard the training ship Mercator, which still lies today as a museum 
ship in the harbor of Ostend. This creates an unexpected connection between Crete, Hawaii and 
Belgium, within the same historical context of disease and exclusion.

### Spinalonga Today

Today, Spinalonga is a protected historical monument and one of the most visited sites in East 
Crete. The island has been made accessible to visitors, but remains at its core a place of 
remembrance.

Spinalonga doesn't tell a simple story. It's about policy and fear, but also about adaptation and 
community. How you experience the island — detached, moving or enlightening — depends heavily on 
the context you bring with you.

<InfoBox title="Practical Tip">
Spinalonga is accessible only by boat. Boats depart daily from Agios Nikolaos, Elounda and Plaka. 
The crossing from Agios Nikolaos takes the longest and is also the most expensive. From Plaka, the 
distance is shortest; this is generally the fastest and most affordable way to reach the island.
</InfoBox>
