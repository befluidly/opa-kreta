_One of the first times we went to Crete, my sister and I were still in school. My parents chose to travel during school holidays because of that. We had no idea that Easter was the biggest celebration for the Greek Orthodox community. Looking back, that exact trip — that experience — was the reason we kept coming back to Crete._

## A Busy Atmosphere at the Hotel

I still remember that all the hotel staff seemed to be in a bit of a rush. It felt busy, but in a 
good way. The receptionist at the time, Foteini, who's now the manager, told us that morning — when 
we wished her good morning — that it was going to be a very special evening.

In Chersonissos (Limenas), there's a small church by the harbor and another one in the center, 
along the main road running through town. Foteini explained that Easter is the biggest celebration 
of the year for Orthodox Greeks, and suggested we go take a look. We had nothing planned that 
Saturday. Mom always had the habit of planning our days in a fixed rhythm: one day of excursions, 
one day at the pool, then excursions again, then the pool again.

## Pool Days, Routine and Small Memories

Our "pool day" wasn't really a day of baking in the sun for twelve hours straight, by the way. We'd 
usually settle in during the morning; by midday we'd go for a walk and have lunch outside the 
hotel, since we had half-board. After lunch, we'd head back to the pool. You'd usually find me in 
the water — I was a real water rat, as my parents used to call me.

Around six, we'd go to our room to shower and get dressed nicely for dinner at the hotel. Back 
then, that meant for the adults: long jeans with a shirt or T-shirt, or a dress down to the knee. 
The kids, of course, were allowed to wear shorts and a T-shirt.

Half-board back then didn't mean everything was served buffet-style either. Usually you could take 
your own starter, but the main course was a choice between fish or meat. Dessert, on the other 
hand, was on the buffet. It all felt a bit more elegant than what you often see now: calmer, with 
less waste and more respect for the staff.

## On Our Way to Church

After dinner, we went back to our room to grab a sweater, since it's not as warm on Crete during 
Easter break as it is in summer. It cools down quite a bit in the evening — a sweater is definitely 
not a bad idea.

The church was barely a five-minute walk from the hotel. One street down, around the corner to the 
left… except we couldn't go left anymore. There was a huge crowd of people! The priest was singing 
the mass through loudspeakers — and it went on for quite a while. We stood among the Greeks, 
curious and excited to be part of this.

Our Easter doesn't always fall on the same date as Orthodox Easter, and in Belgium, Easter isn't 
really a big celebration. For us, it's just a day off — maybe some chocolate eggs for the kids, and 
that's about it.

## A Sea of Light

We soon noticed everyone was holding long, thin candles (not yet lit), often with a little guard so 
the wax wouldn't drip onto their hands, and ribbons tied around them. And there we stood: four 
Belgians in a sea of Greeks. Without candles. We didn't stay unnoticed for long.

Before we knew it, a few Greeks pressed candles into our hands too, so we could join in. We had no 
idea what they were for, but we accepted them with a big smile and a sense of wonder at how 
spontaneously we were welcomed into this. Beautiful!

The priest stopped singing and came outside carrying a lit candle. The church sits on a small hill, 
so we had a clear view of everything. Before we realized it, the Greeks were passing the flame to 
each other. It was a long chain of little flames slowly spreading through the crowd.

The people in front of us turned around, said something in Greek and lit our candles. In turn, we 
turned around too, to pass the light further along. What beautiful symbolism. What a powerful 
tradition. And that hospitality… it still moves me when I think back on it.

## Fireworks and Sparks

We looked back toward the church and saw a fire being lit on top. The Judas effigy burned brightly, 
and then the fireworks began. I've never in my life heard such powerful bangs. The ground shook, 
the sound was deafening, but the display of light was magnificent.

It was only years later that I heard towns compete with each other over who has the most beautiful 
fireworks. This is a celebration you really need to experience once on Crete — or anywhere in 
Greece, for that matter.

After the fireworks, we headed back to the hotel, where we had to hand in our candles. Safety 
first — good thing, too.

## The Next Morning

The next morning, Easter Sunday, we were given deep red, hard-boiled eggs at breakfast. In the 
Orthodox tradition, these red Easter eggs symbolize the blood of Christ, shed on the cross for the 
redemption of humanity, and his resurrection from the tomb.

We watched some of the staff, each holding an egg, try to tap them against each other without 
breaking them. That little game, where the eggs knock against one another, celebrates the 
resurrection of Jesus. The hard eggshell represents the sealed tomb of Christ, and cracking it 
represents his rising from it.

## An Invitation We Never Forgot

That day, on Foteini's advice and despite mom's planning, we decided to stay around the hotel. We 
were still so full of the experience from the night before that Foteini invited us to celebrate 
Easter Sunday lunch with her and her family.

While we waited, we wandered around a bit. On the beach, there were no sunbeds left, but rows of 
improvised barbecues, neatly lined up along the entire length of the beach. At each barbecue sat a 
man in a chair, turning the spit. On the spit — vegetarians and vegans might want to brace 
themselves — lambs.

Crete is known for its lamb dishes; it's simply part of the culture here. In the Orthodox 
tradition, the lamb symbolizes Jesus Christ as the "Lamb of God," who sacrificed himself for the 
sins of humanity.

## "Opa!"

My father, always the joker, knew exactly one word of Greek: "Opa!" (hence the name of this 
website too). As we walked back toward the hotel, we passed an alley where a group of Greeks sat 
around a barbecue, with some raki. Dad couldn't help himself and shouted "OPA!" loudly.

The men laughed and called for him to join them. Said and done. Two minutes later he came back 
with a cup of raki in one hand and a leg of lamb in the other — looking exactly like a caveman 
returning from the hunt. Wonderful!

That feeling of hospitality can't really be described — you just have to experience it.

## Lunch with Foteini's Family

Around two, we went back to the hotel for the festive meal (Greeks eat their lunch a good bit 
later than we do). Part of the family was already there. Foteini came to meet us, took us along and 
gave us a seat at the long table with her family.

We Belgians usually feel a bit awkward when we're invited by strangers — let alone in a country 
where we don't speak the language. But what a wonderful meal! Food and drink in abundance. We ate 
until we were completely full and even tried our hand at a few Greek dances. The afternoon flew by.

We skipped dinner that day — we'd eaten enough for two days.

## A Memory That Stays

What a wonderful experience. Since then, I've only experienced Orthodox Easter once more. I hope 
to experience it again someday, but so far it hasn't happened.

This experience was so special for our family that we kept coming back to Crete. To this day, we 
still talk about it. And I can't stress enough how grateful I am to have been able to experience 
this.

Sometimes I think that back then, between the candlelight and the shouts of "Opa!," I left a piece 
of my heart on Crete.
