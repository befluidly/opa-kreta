\*Everyone knows Crete is a popular summer destination. The largest Greek island has plenty to 
offer, of course: sun, sea and beach, but also unspoiled nature with hiking trails, archaeological 
sites, hundreds — if not thousands — of chapels and monasteries, museums, culture and endless 
character.\*

\*What people rarely think of when it comes to Crete is winter. Yes, winter! Yet the island has 
everything it needs to be a fascinating destination then too.\*

\*For three years now, I've been visiting Crete in winter, usually in January. Not to lie on the 
beach or lounge by the pool, of course, but precisely because the island shows something different 
then: peace, authenticity and an unexpected charm.\*

\*Heraklion in January is a city that finds its own rhythm again, without the bustle of cruise 
ships and tourists. The sea remains close by, but life takes place more indoors.\*

## Why I Decided to Visit Crete in Winter

Why did I decide to visit Crete in winter?

Well, at the time I was seriously considering making Crete my new home — even though it already 
felt that way, really. I wanted to move my life there.

Back then, and honestly still now, I had a hard time holding back my tears on the flight home to 
Belgium. It's as if with every visit, I leave a little more of my heart behind on the island.

A friend — a Cretan woman who worked at the taverna where I went for coffee every day — noticed I 
was struggling. We got to talking. She asked me why I didn't just come to Crete in winter sometime, 
since the island is completely different from how it is in summer.

She said I might like living there less if I didn't know what a Cretan winter really feels like. 
She then suggested I stay with her and her family in [Heraklion](/gidsen/heraklion/bezoek-heraklion).

And so I booked a round-trip flight to Crete, in winter.

The countdown could begin again — much to my colleagues' annoyance. First Christmas and New Year's 
to get through, and before I knew it, the day had come.

Flying to Crete in winter isn't really any different from summer. There are two Greek airlines 
that fly almost daily: Aegean Airlines and Sky Express. The only difference is that in winter 
there's always a layover in Athens. SN Brussels also flies to Athens, after which you transfer to 
another airline (most likely one of the two Greek ones).

I don't mind a layover in Athens. I've been there so often by now that it almost feels familiar. 
Every once in a while I even run into Greek friends who happen to be waiting for their connection 
too. A coffee, a bit of wandering around, and before you know it you're boarding again. By the way: 
Athens is a pretty pleasant airport.

An hour later, you're standing on Cretan soil again. Sometimes I feel like dropping to my knees and 
kissing the ground, like the pope, but I always manage to hold myself back.

## Heraklion

Once inside the old airport building, you immediately notice how quiet and small the airport 
actually is. Not a soul in sight, except for the sixty passengers from my flight. No other 
arrivals, no lines, no crowds.

I didn't have to wait for luggage, since I was only traveling with eight kilos of carry-on. That's 
another advantage of traveling to Crete in winter: you need far fewer clothes! (More on that in a 
moment.)

Outside the airport building, my friend's partner and daughter were already waiting for me. It was 
dark by then, of course. Just like at home, the days are shorter there in winter.

After catching up and exchanging gifts, I got to settle into the son's room — who had to sleep on 
the big couch for a week (oops!).

I decided to simply join in with my friend's family's daily life. She had seventeen-year-old twins 
and a four-year-old daughter at the time.

I won't go into detail about their lives in this post; I mainly want to give you an idea of what 
Crete is like in winter.

## Life in Heraklion in Winter

What you need to understand is that the tourist season completely winds down from late October 
onward. The hotels close, as do most restaurants and taverns in the touristy resort towns. 
Heraklion isn't a resort town, though — it's the island's capital, and life there simply carries 
on, albeit at a slower pace than in summer.

No overcrowded buses, no lines. The [museums](/gidsen/heraklion/musea-van-heraklion) are empty, 
apart from a few students and school groups required to visit. The archaeological sites like 
[Knossos](/gidsen/heraklion/knossos) and Phaistos also remain open, though with adjusted opening 
hours.

What does a weekday look like in the capital during winter? Not that different from back home. 
Getting up, getting ready, dropping the kids off at school, and… coffee in the center. Not heading 
to work — at least not for the Cretans who work in the tourism sector.

Some might pick up extra income here and there in winter, but often they don't work from late 
October to early April. The money they earn in summer is what carries them through the winter. Not 
always easy, since their wages are often peanuts compared to what we earn. And yet, they always 
manage.

After coffee — or several coffees — there's time for a walk or to browse the local market for fresh 
vegetables, cheese, clothing or meat. Then back home to make lunch, do some household chores, and 
around 2pm the kids are picked up from school. Lunch, siesta, homework.

We Belgians usually don't join in on that midday nap tradition, so I often went for walks with the 
family's dog instead. Wonderfully peaceful, with the sun on my face.

In the afternoon, I helped the twins with their homework, since the education system there isn't 
exactly great. Since my second language is French and my third is German, I was able to help them 
with their language subjects. Dinner is simple: some bread with salad, a wrap, or — just like at 
home — sometimes takeout.

## My Impression of the Capital in Winter

I personally think Heraklion is the most beautiful of the three big cities (the others being 
Chania and Rethymno). There's plenty to do, and over the twenty years since my first visit, the 
city has gradually changed: more pleasant, easier to navigate, and especially more 
pedestrian-friendly.

A walking path has been built along the Venetian walls, squares and buildings are being steadily 
restored, and everything stays remarkably tidy.

In the capital, you'll find numerous [museums](/gidsen/heraklion/musea-van-heraklion), such as the 
Heraklion Archaeological Museum, the Historical Museum of Crete and the Natural History Museum. On 
top of that, there's the harbor, the covered market, charming little squares, narrow streets, 
churches and, of course, shops — basically everything you'd need for a short city trip.

The atmosphere in winter is calm, relaxed. No rushed hustle like in summer. People sit outside 
their homes on chairs until late, kids still play in the street. Neighbors stop for a chat or just 
pull up a chair.

In the morning or afternoon, people meet up for coffee, and on weekends they head into town to eat 
at one of the many cozy taverns in the center.

On Sundays, the barbecue often gets fired up — yes, even in winter! Every year I get to brag to 
family, friends and colleagues that I've already had my first barbecue of the year by the time I 
get home in January. Hehe.

## The Temperature in January

I obviously can't speak for the entire winter, but in January the climate is mild Mediterranean, 
just like we learned in school. Mild temperatures, alternating sun and clouds. The sky is more 
often blue than gray.

With temperatures around 17 degrees during the day, it's lovely to sit on a terrace or go for a 
walk. Mornings are still crisp, and in the evening it cools down to around 12 degrees. The wind can 
make it feel a bit colder, but overall it's pleasant.

## What Do You Pack in 8 kg of Carry-On?

Besides the jeans I'm wearing when I leave, I pack two more in my bag. Then four T-shirts, three 
sweaters, socks, underwear and an extra pair of sneakers. I usually already wear a jacket when I 
leave, and often a cap or beanie too.

On top of that: pajamas, cloth handkerchiefs, medication, a hairbrush, toothbrush, cables and 
chargers. For a week, 8 kg is really enough.

I don't bring soap or toiletries — I have those on-site with the family. And if you're staying in 
an Airbnb, that's usually already provided too. If needed, you can just buy what you need at the 
supermarket.

## Final Thought

Crete in winter isn't a destination, it's a feeling. Peace, warmth, good coffee and people who have 
time to talk. No beach towel or sunscreen needed — just a jacket, a smile and a heart that's open.

And yes, I eat my first barbecue of the year in January. On Crete. 😄
