\*Crete is known for its impressive [gorges](/en/gidsen/natuur-en-wandelen/de-mooiste-kloven). Tourists 
flock in large numbers to the Samaria Gorge, the Imbros Gorge, the Zakros Gorge and the Aradena 
Gorge. But alongside these well-known names, there are also hidden gems that see far fewer 
visitors. One of them is the Trypiti Gorge.\*

\*The Trypiti Gorge isn't a classic gorge for hiking. It's actually a short gravel road on a small 
plateau, close to sea level. The road is only a few hundred meters long and just wide enough for 
one car. Still, the spot is special: the route ends at Trypiti Beach, a remote little beach only 
reachable via unpaved roads.\*

_And that's exactly where our unforgettable adventure began._

## The Start of Our Adventure

It was one of those days you never forget. As so often, my mother had carefully mapped out the 
route. Back then, we still rented a minibus for 9 to 11 people, big enough for our whole group.

We set off early, after a hearty breakfast, heading toward the south of the island. The road from 
Heraklion to the [Messara Plain](/en/gidsen/heraklion/de-messaravlakte) hadn't been renovated yet at 
the time, so the drive took at least an hour. Along the way, we made a short stop at Lake Zaros. 
There we could stretch our legs and grab something to drink before continuing on.

The route to the Trypiti Gorge first took us over the mountains. Once at the top, we came out onto 
a dry, barren plain. The road was still reasonably passable, though we knew it could get worse. But 
at the summit, things changed: the asphalt ended and turned into an unpaved gravel road.

Keep in mind we were in a minibus full of people. Luckily, my father, who works as a bus driver in 
everyday life, was behind the wheel. With a steady hand, he drove our minibus down the mountain, 
toward the gorge.

## A Well-Deserved Break at Agisilaos–Stella Taverna

After descending for about half an hour, we decided to take a break. We came upon a small taverna: 
Agisilaos–Stella Taverna. The spot was simple but charming. There were a few plastic tables and 
chairs, just enough for our group. Everything sat beneath grapevines, while colorful flowers 
decorated the edges. The view was beautiful: the mountains, the sun, the wild 
[olive trees](/en/gidsen/eten-en-drinken/bezoek-olijfboeren) and the silence of nature.

The owner came out to greet us enthusiastically, visibly happy that visitors had stopped by. In 
his best English, he took our order, and a little later brought not just our drinks, but also a 
plate of fresh tomatoes, cucumbers, olives and feta — on the house, just like that. That's Cretan 
hospitality at its purest. My parents, who've visited several Greek islands, always say Crete is 
the most hospitable island of all. And this was the perfect proof of that.

After half an hour of enjoying the view and the quiet, we decided to continue on our way. And then 
the real adventure began.

## The Drama in the Gorge

Less than five minutes after setting off, we drove over a large, loose chunk of rock, at least 30 
to 40 centimeters high. The minibus drove over it as if nothing had happened, but my father 
immediately noticed the steering felt strange.

Once on the plateau, right before the entrance to the gorge, we decided to stop to take photos. 
When everyone was about to get back in, my father asked me to watch the wheels while he slowly 
drove forward. And sure enough: the right rear wheel was wobbling all over the place. The axle was 
broken. There we were. In the middle of the heat, with no signal, no sign of life anywhere. The sun 
was blazing and there was no shade to be found. We had no choice: we had to walk uphill to find 
help.

After about ten minutes, we finally saw an old pickup truck approaching. It was a shepherd. Using 
hands, feet and our limited Greek, we tried to explain that we'd broken down. He understood us 
immediately and pointed toward a small taverna beyond the gorge. He let us climb into the truck bed 
and drove us there in two trips. Meanwhile, he took my mother to his house on top of the hill, 
where she was able to contact the car rental company via a landline.

## I Xaroupia: Pure Cretan Hospitality

The taverna we ended up at was called Η Χαρούπια (I Xaroupia). A simple, remote little place, run 
by a woman and her family. Behind the house they kept goats, sheep and chickens, and a small 
vegetable garden supplied them with produce. Here, everything on the table came straight from their 
own land.

When she heard what had happened, the owner immediately brought us extra water. She told us she 
had a pot of μοσχάρι (moschari) on the stove that day — a stew of tender beef with tomatoes, herbs, 
onions and olive oil, served with spaghetti. A dish Greeks often eat at home, but rarely find on a 
menu.

Half an hour later, we were served generously filled plates. The taste was heavenly: slow-cooked 
meat, a rich tomato sauce, everything pure and fresh. We ate as if we were starving. When the owner 
saw how much we were enjoying it, she even came back to give us extra helpings. After the meal, she 
brought out her lyra and played music while she sang. There we sat, bellies full, surrounded by 
hospitality, music and the warmth of real Cretan culture.

## The Rescue

Meanwhile, the shepherd stayed patiently with us until the rescue crew arrived. After a couple of 
hours, the car rental company showed up with a new minibus and a tow truck to pick up the broken 
one. A risky job, since towing a vehicle down the mountain on gravel roads is no small feat. The 
rental company even had to pay extra to the towing service because of the risk involved. But in the 
end, they managed to get the minibus down safely.

After this adventure, we decided to head back to the hotel. Luckily, the alternative route was 
better paved and less dangerous, though it remained an exciting drive.

## An Unexpected Discovery

It was only much later that we heard a detail that made this adventure even more special. Years 
earlier, my mother's cousin had been in this same area, back when she was chaperoning young people 
on a camp trip in the 
[region of Kalamaki, Kommos and Matala](/en/gidsen/heraklion/de-messaravlakte). During that trip, she 
took a photo with a shepherd: a sturdy Cretan man, in a black shirt with rolled-up sleeves, a net 
around his head, gray pants and the typical shepherd boots. An impressive figure.

And you guessed it: that same shepherd was the one who came to our rescue years later in the 
Trypiti Gorge. What an incredible coincidence — as if Crete let us relive a piece of family history.

## What We Learned

This adventure may have been reckless — a minibus simply isn't suited for off-roading — but we 
had no regrets. Without that breakdown, we would never have experienced this remarkable adventure 
and that unique hospitality.

What we take away from this:

- **Never go off-road with a minibus.** Choose a 4x4 or SUV instead.
- **Always take out full insurance** when renting a car, including coverage for damage to the 
underside.
- **You won't find authentic Crete in the resort towns**, but in small villages and remote 
taverns.
- **Cretan hospitality is unique.** We've never experienced anything like it so intensely 
anywhere else in Europe.

## Conclusion

The Trypiti Gorge isn't a [gorge](/en/gidsen/natuur-en-wandelen/de-mooiste-kloven) in the way you 
might expect. It's not a long hike like Samaria or Imbros, but a short gravel road that ends at a 
beautifully remote beach. For us, it became an adventure full of tension, hospitality and 
unexpected encounters. A memory that will stay with us forever. This is the real Crete.
